“We need a dashboard” sounds like a clear request. It usually isn’t.

A dashboard is a possible answer. Somewhere underneath the request is a decision someone is trying to make, a worry they can’t quite name, or a conversation that keeps going in circles.

The useful work starts there.

## Start with the decision

Before opening a tool, try finishing this sentence:

> When I see this information, I will decide whether to ______.

It’s a small exercise, but it exposes a surprising amount. If the blank is difficult to fill, the team may need a conversation before it needs a new data product.

Consider a request to track customer activity. It could mean three very different things:

| The question | The possible decision |
| --- | --- |
| Are new customers getting started? | Change the onboarding experience |
| Are established customers becoming less active? | Reach out before a renewal |
| Which features are being used? | Reconsider a product investment |

The same word, *activity*, hides different definitions, audiences, and time horizons.

## Make the ambiguity visible

A good question creates space for specifics. Who will use the answer? How often does the decision happen? What would change someone’s mind?

Write down the assumptions alongside the metric. A simple definition that another person can challenge is more useful than an impressive calculation that nobody can explain.

This is also where [documentation becomes part of the product](/writing/documentation-is-part-of-the-product/), rather than something saved for the end.

## Build the smallest useful answer

Sometimes the first answer is a query. Sometimes it’s a spreadsheet or a sketch shared in a meeting. The important thing is whether it helps someone make the decision.

A smaller answer is easier to question and cheaper to change. Once it proves useful, you can decide what deserves a pipeline, a model, and an ongoing owner.

Good data work begins with curiosity. The tools come next.
